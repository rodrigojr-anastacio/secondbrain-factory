## Bloco 1: hook (0:00–0:45)

### B01 [0:00] The page
This page was typed in June 1942, and somebody stamped it SECRET. It's Admiral Chester Nimitz's report to Washington on a battle three weeks old. Read line (a). "4 CV sunk – Akagi, Kaga, Soryu, Hiryu – with the loss of all their planes and many of their personnel." Four Japanese aircraft carriers, gone. Now do the math at the bottom. "Ninety-two officers and two hundred and fifteen men." The American cost. Three hundred and seven. Remember that number. The subject line reads: Battle of Midway. That place is still out there, in the middle of the Pacific. And you're not allowed to go.

## Bloco 2: âncora humana + pergunta do título (0:45–1:45)

### B02 [0:41] The place
Midway Atoll is a ring of coral more than twelve hundred miles northwest of Honolulu. About forty people live there. Nobody else gets to land. And two things there are very strange. A runway that's still kept ready, in case a jet full of passengers gets into trouble over the Pacific. And a bird that was already nesting here in 1956, when a scientist put a band on her leg. She's still coming back. Keep that year in mind.

### B03 [1:12] The last man
In 2017, two old Marines laid a wreath on Midway. One was John Miniclier. On June 4th, 1942, he was a young private on Sand Island, on top of a wooden searchlight tower, counting Japanese planes. In 2025, he died at a hundred and three, the last known survivor of the Battle of Midway. So why? Why can't you visit Midway Atoll, the place where the war in the Pacific turned? It isn't a military secret. The Navy left decades ago. It isn't forgotten. It's a national memorial. And it isn't that nobody wants to go. For years, people flew in to see it. What keeps you out is a chain of decisions, a few million birds, and an island that's slowly falling apart. Along the way, you'll hear about a fake message about drinking water that told America where Japan would strike, a Hollywood director knocked out on a rooftop, and what a robot found three miles down.

## Bloco 3: o que aconteceu ali (~35%)

### B04 [2:15] A wire, a fleet, an airline
To understand why anybody would fight over two specks of sand, start with a wire. In 1903, a cable company built a station on Sand Island. It was the final link in a telegraph line across the Pacific, and President Theodore Roosevelt used it to send a message. That same year, a presidential order put the atoll under the United States Navy. In 1935, Pan American Airways arrived. Its big flying boats, the Clippers, landed in Midway's lagoon on the way to Asia, and Pan Am built a small hotel on the sand. Then the Navy started building for real: dredging the lagoon, a seaplane base on Sand Island, runways on Eastern Island. This photo was taken on November 24th, 1941. Look how finished it is. Two weeks later, on the same day as Pearl Harbor, Japanese warships shelled Midway too. A Marine officer killed in that attack, George Cannon, became the first Marine to earn the Medal of Honor in World War Two.

**Tela:** KenBurns do prédio da estação do cabo (Commons "Figure 13- Cable Station Building, Mess Hall", 2015, levantamento federal; L-H2); DocGrifo da EO 13022 na linha "Executive Order 199-A of January 20, 1903" (prova da jurisdição da Marinha); foto de época do Clipper/Pan Am em Midway (L-E1, A CONFERIR licença); Mapa2D da rota do Clipper (São Francisco → Honolulu → Midway → Wake → Guam → Manila); **EntaoAgora** aérea de 24/11/1941 (#6) × ISS 2001 (#22), rótulos "1941" / "2001". Ficha da #6: "NOV 24 1941 · MIDWAY ATOLL · U.S. Navy 80-G-451086, National Archives".

### B05 [3:21] "AF"
By the spring of 1942, Japan had been winning for six months. Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto wanted to lure the American carriers into one big fight and finish them. The bait would be Midway. But at Pearl Harbor, a Navy codebreaking unit called Station Hypo was reading parts of the Japanese navy's code. The intercepts kept pointing to a target called "AF." The unit's commander, Joseph Rochefort, was sure AF was Midway. Washington wasn't convinced. So one of his staff, Jasper Holmes, came up with a trick. The instructions went to Midway over the undersea cable, where Japan couldn't listen. Then Midway sent a fake radio message, in the clear: its fresh-water plant had broken down. Japanese listeners took the bait and passed the news along. Hypo decoded that, too. AF was short of water. The Navy's own historians add a twist. The trick wasn't really meant to convince Nimitz. It was meant to convince Washington.

**Tela:** KenBurns retrato de Yamamoto (NHHC, A CONFERIR id) e de Rochefort (NSA/USN, L-E2); Selo "SCHEMATIC": cartela datilografada "MIDWAY → PEARL (UNDERSEA CABLE)" e "MIDWAY → ALL (IN THE CLEAR): WATER PLANT FAILURE" (texto gerado em código, não documento fingido); DocGrifo da página NHHC H-006-2 na frase "a phony radio message in the clear saying that its fresh water–making capability was broken"; Número "AF".

### B06 [4:23] Five degrees, five miles, five minutes
Nimitz already believed it. His intelligence officer, Edwin Layton, even predicted where the Japanese carriers would first be spotted: bearing 325 degrees, 175 nautical miles from Midway, at six in the morning on June 4th. Nimitz later said Layton was off by only "five degrees, five miles, and five minutes." The Navy's historians say it was a little more than that, but not by much. So three American carriers were waiting northeast of the island. Yorktown, Enterprise and Hornet. On the morning of June 4th, 108 Japanese planes came for Midway. The Marines were ready. Every plane on the atoll that could fly was already in the air. The fighting over the islands themselves lasted about half an hour.

Opcional: DocGrifo da Track Chart (#16).

### B07 [5:10] The rooftop
One of the men watching from a rooftop was a Hollywood director. John Ford was now a Navy commander with a movie camera. He'd been posted on top of the power house on Sand Island, with phones wired to the command post. He had his camera lined up on a hangar, guessing it would be one of the first things hit. He was right. In his own words: "A Zero flew about 50 feet over it and dropped a bomb and hit it, the whole thing went up. I was knocked unconscious." He came to, and he got the shot. It's in the film he made that year, *The Battle of Midway*. This is the seaplane hangar on Sand Island after the raid. Remember that hangar. Across the island, John Miniclier was still up on his searchlight tower, counting. He counted about thirty-two planes overhead.

**Tela:** Filme 6 s do *The Battle of Midway* (#1), explosões no atol (sem o som original); DocGrifo da transcrição oral de Ford no NHHC ("John Ford Remembers Filming Battle of Midway") com grifo na citação; Ficha "JUN 4 1942 · SAND ISLAND · U.S. Navy film, National Archives"; KenBurns do hangar danificado (#8) com Ficha "JUN 4 1942 · SEAPLANE HANGAR, SAND ISLAND · U.S. Navy, PD"; Número "32". **[P1 plantada]**

### B08 [6:08] Torpedo Squadron Eight
Out at sea, the American counterattack was going badly. Wave after wave of torpedo planes found the Japanese carriers, and wave after wave was shot down. From the three American carriers, forty-one torpedo bombers went in. Six came back. Torpedo Squadron Eight, from the Hornet, lost all fifteen of its planes. Its skipper, John Waldron, had told his men that if only one plane was left, he wanted that man to go in and get a hit. The last plane was flown by Ensign George Gay. He dropped his torpedo at the carrier Soryu. It missed, and he was shot down. Gay was the only man in that attack to survive. This is him a few days later, recovering in a hospital bed.

**Tela:** Filme 5 s de *Torpedo Squadron 8* (#2, Ford, USN); Número "41 → 6"; KenBurns de George Gay no hospital (#14, NARA 80-G-17678) com Ficha "JUNE 1942 · ENS. GEORGE GAY, VT-8 · National Archives, Unrestricted"; Mapa2D simples das ondas de ataque (setas amarelas) sobre a mesma base do B06.

### B09 [6:57] Five minutes
But those sacrifices kept the Japanese carriers busy. Their crews were launching fighters, landing them, and swerving to dodge torpedoes. Nobody had time to get a counterstrike into the air. Then, at 10:20 that morning, forty-eight American dive bombers showed up overhead. Two squadrons from Enterprise and one from Yorktown arrived at almost the same moment, by pure coincidence. In about five minutes, Akagi, Kaga and Soryu were wrecks on fire. The fourth carrier, Hiryu, hit back. Her planes found the Yorktown and left her badly damaged and listing. That afternoon, American dive bombers caught Hiryu too. By the next morning, all four Japanese carriers had been sunk by their own side's torpedoes, so the Americans couldn't capture them.

**Tela:** Número "10:20" → contador "5 MIN"; KenBurns do Yorktown atingido (#9 e #10, NARA 80-G-414423) com Ficha "JUN 4 1942 · USS YORKTOWN (CV-5) · National Archives, Unrestricted"; KenBurns do Hiryu em chamas, 5/6/1942 (#11, NH 73064/73065) com Ficha "JUN 5 1942 · HIRYU ADRIFT · NHHC, PD"; Filme 4 s (#4, cópia CC0 no IA).

### B10 [7:45] Line (c)
The Yorktown almost made it. Her crew kept her afloat for two days, under tow toward Pearl Harbor. Then, on June 6th, the Japanese submarine I-168 put torpedoes into the Yorktown and the destroyer Hammann beside her. Hammann sank almost at once. Yorktown went down the next morning. Japan also lost the heavy cruiser Mikuma. Her sister ship, Mogami, was badly damaged, but she got away. Now go back to Nimitz's page. Line (c). "2 CA sunk – Mogami, Mikuma." One of those two cruisers didn't sink. Hold on to that. The page also estimates that Japan lost 4,800 men. Navy historians today say more than 3,000, including over 700 of the mechanics who kept their planes flying. All 248 of the carriers' planes were lost. After six months of winning, Japan's carrier fleet had finally been stopped, and Japan never got those ships back.

## Bloco 4: virada, por que fechou (~25%)

### B11 [8:44] The base that stayed
Japan never took Midway. But the strangest part of this story starts after the shooting stops. The Navy didn't leave. In 1957, a forty-million-dollar building program began on Sand Island. Midway became home to the Pacific end of America's early-warning line, the radar net meant to spot an attack long before it reached the mainland. At one time, the atoll supported more than 5,000 people. Then the Cold War ended. In 1993, Naval Air Facility Midway Island closed. And the question became: what do you do with a little city in the middle of the ocean that nobody needs to defend anymore?

**Tela:** KenBurns de Midway em 1945 (#7) e foto da era Guerra Fria (avião de alerta aéreo antecipado em Midway, NHHC/NARA, L-E3, A CONFERIR); Número "5,000+"; Mapa2D com a "barreira" Pacífica (linha tracejada amarela, Selo "SCHEMATIC"); Número "1993".

### B12 [9:25] Executive Order 13022
On October 31st, 1996, President Bill Clinton signed Executive Order 13022. It moved Midway from the Navy to the Department of the Interior. Read section three. The Fish and Wildlife Service "shall administer the Midway Islands as the Midway Atoll National Wildlife Refuge." Now look at the first purpose on its list: "maintaining and restoring natural biological diversity within the refuge." History comes fifth: "recognize and maintain the historic significance of the Midway Islands." So the island's new boss was a wildlife agency, and on paper, the birds came first. The last Navy personnel left the next year. In 2000, the refuge was also named the Battle of Midway National Memorial. And for a while, you could still go. Small groups flew in for wildlife and history tours.

**Tela:** DocGrifo da EO 13022 no Federal Register (61 FR 56875, 4/11/1996): página → grifo em Sec. 3(a) → grifo em (1) → grifo em (5); Ficha "OCT 31 1996 · WASHINGTON · Federal Register, PD"; Número "1996"; KenBurns de albatrozes ao lado de prédios antigos (USFWS, L-H3); KenBurns do memorial/placa (USFWS #23 ou #25).

### B13 [10:16] The paint
But the Navy left something behind besides runways. Many of the buildings on Midway were finished with lead-based paint. As the paint flaked, it ended up in the soil, and albatross chicks nesting right next to the walls swallowed it. In 2014, a Fish and Wildlife official told Congress: "Lead poisoning has caused the mortality of approximately 10,000 albatross chicks annually, creating a conservation crisis." Ten thousand chicks a year. Starting in 2011, the Service has been scraping, sealing and tearing down. Of the old cable station buildings that were shedding paint, three were removed because they were too far gone to save. A federal audit later counted seven historic buildings demolished as part of the lead cleanup.

**Tela:** DocGrifo do depoimento de Kevin Foerster (USFWS) à Câmara, 20/11/2014 (doi.gov) com grifo na citação; Número "10,000 / YEAR"; KenBurns de prédio descascando ou da estação do cabo (L-H2); DocGrifo curto do relatório GAO-16-382 na frase "demolished seven historic properties"; Ficha "NOV 20 2014 · U.S. HOUSE SUBCOMMITTEE · DOI, PD".

### B14 [11:03] The money
Then came the money. Running Midway is expensive. Food, fuel and building materials come from Hawaii or the mainland. A single barge of supplies can cost about 760,000 dollars per trip. For fiscal years 2012 and 2013, the Service cut Midway's budget by more than a million dollars. A report by the Government Accountability Office spells out what happened next. Officials said the lower budget "led to suspension of public visitation on Midway in November 2012." The same report found that the building used to house visitors was "almost beyond the point of repair and renovation." To bring visitors back, the Service estimated it would need 1.2 million dollars a year. Today, the government's own page for the monument says it plainly. "Historical and eco-tour access is currently not offered."

## Bloco 5: o que ainda está lá (~25%)

### B15 [11:55] One more reason
So the visitors stopped. One thing didn't. The runway, Henderson Field, is still maintained as an emergency landing site. In the Service's own words to Congress, it's there "for nearly 35,000 jet aircraft transiting the Pacific Ocean." But there's one more reason Midway stays closed, and you can see it in the buildings. Remember the seaplane hangar, the one hit on the morning of June 4th, 1942? After the battle, only half of it was rebuilt. For eighty years, salt, wind and rain worked on it. In January 2022, after weeks of storms, the Fish and Wildlife Service posted this. "Sadly, the seaplane hangar collapsed on January 13 during a torrential downpour." Same building. Then and now. And the water is rising. The Service says sea level at Midway is going up about five millimeters a year, faster than the global average.

**Tela:** KenBurns de Henderson Field (L-H1) + DocGrifo do depoimento na linha dos 35.000 jatos; **EntaoAgora** hangar 4/6/1942 (#8) × hangar desabando 2022 (#19, USFWS), rótulos "1942" / "2022"; DocGrifo da matéria USFWS (18/01/2022) com grifo na frase; Número "5 MM / YEAR". **[P1 paga; gancho da pista (B02) pago]**

### B16 [12:52] March 11th, 2011
Midway sits barely above the sea, and in 2011, the sea came over it. On March 11th, an earthquake off Japan sent a tsunami across the Pacific. It washed completely over Spit Island and covered about sixty percent of Eastern Island. No people were hurt. The birds weren't so lucky. Counting the tsunami and two winter storms just before it, the Service found that more than 110,000 albatross chicks were lost. About twenty-two percent of that year's chicks. And at least two thousand adults.

**Tela:** KenBurns das fotos do tsunami (Commons "Midway Laysan albatross 2011 tsunami", "Midway muddy albatross chick 2011 tsunami", USFWS, L-H4); Mapa2D Japão → Midway com a onda (linha amarela) e rótulo "MAR 11 2011"; Número "110,000+"; Número "22%".

### B17 [13:26] The bird from 1956
Now, that bird from 1956. Her name is Wisdom. She's a Laysan albatross. In 1956, a federal scientist named Chandler Robbins put a band on her leg while she sat on an egg. He guessed she was at least five years old. That makes her at least seventy-five now, the oldest known wild bird in the world. She outlived the lead paint, the tsunami and the storms. In late 2024, she laid another egg, with a new mate. It hatched, but the chick didn't survive. In November 2025, she flew in again, and the Service announced it with three words: "The queen returns!" Biologists estimate she's laid fifty to sixty eggs in her life.

**Tela:** KenBurns de Wisdom (Commons 53376481735 e 53376036996, USFWS, PD) com Ficha "DEC 3 2023 · SAND ISLAND · USFWS Pacific, PD"; Número "1956" → "75+"; KenBurns de Chandler Robbins (USGS, L-H5, A CONFERIR); Número "50–60 EGGS". **[Gancho do pássaro (B02) pago]**

### B18 [14:11] The strangest thing on Midway
Every chick on Midway is fed by its parents, with whatever they scoop off the ocean. And the ocean is full of plastic. The Service estimates adult albatrosses bring about five tons of plastic to the atoll every year. Almost all chicks hatched on Midway have plastic inside them. Then there's the strangest thing still living there. The rats were wiped out back in 1996. The house mice were not. Around ten years ago, those mice started attacking nesting albatrosses, birds with wings longer than a man is tall, while they sat on their eggs. In July 2023, crews dropped poison bait from the air across Sand Island to wipe the mice out. A month later, the project admitted it hadn't worked. "Mice are still present in many areas of the island, and a root cause is not immediately evident." It's one more reason every person and every crate that lands on Midway needs a permit.

Sem imagem de ferimento (adequação a anunciantes).

### B19 [15:14] Three miles down
And the battle itself? It's still out there, just not on the island. Remember line (a). Four carriers sunk. For decades, nobody knew exactly where they lay. In 1998, an expedition led by Robert Ballard, with the Navy and National Geographic, found the Yorktown, about three miles down. In October 2019, the research ship Petrel found the Kaga at around 5,400 meters, more than 17,000 feet. Days later, it found the Akagi. In September 2023, the ship Nautilus sent a robot down to all three. The dives went below 5,100 meters, the deepest that ship had ever worked. On the decks, as one of the scientists put it, "many anti-aircraft guns were still pointing up." And line (c)? There's no Mogami down there. She survived Midway. The page was wrong about her. The ocean keeps a better record than the paper does.

## Bloco 6: fecho (~1:30)

### B20 [16:10] So why
So why can't you visit Midway? Because in 1996 it stopped being a Navy base and became a wildlife refuge, and the order that did it put the birds first. Because it's more than twelve hundred miles from Honolulu, and every person, meal and nail has to be flown or shipped in. Because the money ran out in 2012, and the building visitors slept in was almost beyond repair. Because the old base is still shedding lead paint and falling down around nearly three million birds. And because the government itself says so: "Funding, conservation, staffing, and safety challenges have caused the Service to suspend visitor services."

**Tela:** Montagem rápida, um "Because" por imagem (8–10 s cada): EO 13022 grifada → Mapa2D Honolulu–Midway → GAO grifado → hangar caído (#19) + albatrozes → DocGrifo do depoimento de 2014 na frase final.

### B21 [16:53] One more time
Go back to Nimitz's page one more time. Ninety-two officers. Two hundred and fifteen men. Three hundred and seven. Eighty-four years later, that is still the official American count. The men who could tell you what that morning looked like are gone now. John Miniclier was the last of them. The seaplane hangar that was hit that morning has fallen in. The carriers are three miles down, guns still pointing at the surface. And every November, on an island almost nobody is allowed to see, a seventy-five-year-old albatross lands on the sand, looks around, and stays a while.

If someone in your family served at Midway, on the island, on the Yorktown, or in the air, tell us their name and where they were in the comments. This is Clearance Required. We go where the record goes. If you want the next one, subscribe.
