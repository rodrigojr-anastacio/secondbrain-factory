## Bloco 1: hook

### B01 [0:00] The rifle on the rock
Look at this photograph. You've almost certainly seen it before. A young soldier lies dead behind a low stone wall, in a nest of boulders at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania, in July of 1863. His head rests on a knapsack. A rifle leans against the rock above him. The man who sold this picture called it "The Home of a Rebel Sharpshooter," and he wrote a story to go with it. How the sharpshooter built this wall with his own hands. How a shell burst over him. How he lay down on his blanket to wait for death. Now look at the rifle again. Remember it. Because two things about this picture are not what they seem. This man was probably not a sharpshooter. And he did not die here.

## Bloco 2: âncora humana + a pergunta do título

### B02 [0:45] The other plates
In 1975, a historian named William Frassanito published a book built on a simple idea. He took the original glass-plate photographs from Gettysburg out to the battlefield and compared them with the ground itself. Rock by rock, tree by tree. And the photographs began to argue with their own captions. So what are the Gettysburg photos you weren't supposed to see? They weren't classified. They weren't censored. Some of them were on sale to the public within months of the battle. The photos you weren't supposed to see are the other plates. The ones taken a few minutes earlier, a few yards away, or from the other side of the same field. The ones that show how the famous pictures were made. The National Archives puts it bluntly: for over 100 years, historians did not question the captions. Along the way, you'll see a body carried across a hillside, the same dead men given to two different armies, and a crowd photograph that kept its secret for eighty-nine years.

**Tela:** Little Round Top / Devil's Den em 1863 e hoje; DocGrifo `doc_nara_2013` na frase "for over 100 years historians did not question the captions"; Número "1975". **Pergunta do título dita em ~1:10.**

## Bloco 3: o que aconteceu ali

### B03 [1:50] Three days in July
In the summer of 1863, the Civil War was in its third year. Robert E. Lee had taken the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia north, into Pennsylvania. On the morning of July 1st, outside a small crossroads town called Gettysburg, his men ran into the Union Army of the Potomac. For three days, the two armies fought across ridges, orchards and wheat fields, around a hill called Little Round Top and a jumble of giant boulders known as the Devil's Den. When it ended, more than fifty-one thousand men were dead, wounded or missing. It was the costliest battle of the war. On the Fourth of July, in the rain, Lee began his retreat. And the people of Gettysburg were left with the dead. In the words of the National Park Service, every farm field or garden was a graveyard.

**Tela:** Mapa2D Gettysburg (Seminary Ridge, Cemetery Ridge, Little Round Top, Devil's Den); fotos de 1863 de Little Round Top, Culp's Hill (árvores destroçadas), seminário; Número "51,000+".

### B04 [2:45] The man who quit Brady
Into those fields, a day or two later, came a wagon full of glass and chemicals. Alexander Gardner was a Scottish-born photographer who had spent years running Mathew Brady's gallery in Washington. Brady was the most famous name in American photography. But Brady had a habit. Pictures his employees made went out to the world as "Photographed by Brady." In May of 1863, Gardner left and opened his own studio in Washington, and he took some of Brady's best people with him. One of them was a young cameraman named Timothy O'Sullivan. On July 5th, two days after the fighting stopped, Gardner and O'Sullivan reached Gettysburg. Brady's own team would arrive later, after most of the dead were already in the ground. For a few days, the most powerful pictures of the war belonged to the man who had quit him.

**Tela:** retrato de Gardner (1863); fachada da galeria de Gardner em Washington; Brady em McPherson's Woods (julho de 1863); Número "JULY 5, 1863".

### B05 [3:35] Ten seconds of stillness
To understand what happened next, you have to understand the camera. In 1863, a photographer coated a sheet of glass with wet chemicals, slid it into the camera, and opened the lens for anywhere from five to twenty seconds. Then he had minutes to develop the plate before it dried, inside a darkroom on wheels. Nothing that moved could be photographed clearly. Not a charge, not a cannon firing, not a battle. The camera could only record what was left when the fighting stopped. And what was left lay perfectly still. Gardner knew his customers. Within months, he was selling these views from his Washington gallery, as large prints and as cards for the stereoscope, the 3D viewer that sat in Victorian parlors. Years later, he would publish the best of them in a two-volume album, Gardner's Photographic Sketch Book of the War, each plate with a page of his own words beside it. Those words are the problem. Remember one more thing he wrote there. He said he came back to Gettysburg on November 19th, 1863. Remember that date.

**Tela:** Cartela SCHEMATIC do processo de colódio úmido (5–20 s); vista estéreo de 1863; folha de rosto do Sketch Book (desenho de Waud); Número "5–20 SEC".

### B06 [4:45] The field by the Rose farm
On July 5th and 6th, Gardner's team worked fast. Near the Rose family's farm, they found the dead of the second day's fighting, many still unburied, some gathered in rows for the burial parties. Look at the far left of this frame. That wagon is very likely the photographers' own, caught by accident. In one open field nearby, O'Sullivan set up his camera in front of a line of bodies stretched across the grass. He made one exposure. Then he moved around to the other side of the same row and made another. The men in these pictures had been stripped of their shoes. In 1863, shoes were precious, and the living took them from the dead. Remember that. The shoes. These were real men, with names their families might never learn. Gardner would give them a name of a different kind.

**Tela:** Rose woods (mortos reunidos para enterro, equipamento de O'Sullivan à esquerda: detalhe); vista da linha de corpos sem close; Número "JULY 5–6, 1863". Sem rosto em close, sem ferimento.

### B07 [5:45] A young man on the slope
On July 6th, the team climbed into the Devil's Den. Here, on the second day of the battle, Confederate soldiers had pushed up through the boulders toward Little Round Top. On the southern slope, the photographers came across the body of one young soldier. Something about him held their attention. At a time when every plate cost time and money, they photographed this one man again and again. They photographed him lying among the rocks, a rifle beside him, a cap behind his head. Then from another angle, and another, one with the boulder-strewn valley called the Slaughter Pen behind him. In all, the Park Service counts six separate images of this one soldier. Four of them were made right here, on the slope where he most likely fell. Which raises a question. If he died here, on the open hillside, how did he end up behind a wall?

**Tela:** Devil's Den 1863 (paisagem); `foto_ultimo_sono_gardner` (prancha 40) e a variante; vista de O'Sullivan com o vale ao fundo; Número "6 PLATES".

### B08 [6:45] Forty yards
Frassanito's answer was in the rocks. In the background of four of the photographs, he could match the boulders to the southern edge of the Devil's Den. In the other two, the famous ones, the background was different: a narrow gap between two huge rocks, closed by a stone wall. That spot is about forty yards away. The body is the same. The face is the same. The uniform is the same. The only explanation that fits is that, after taking their pictures on the slope, the photographers noticed that dramatic little nook, carried the dead man there, and photographed him again. In one stereo view of the new setting, you can see a blanket under the body. It may be what they used to carry him. And then they made their picture. A body in a nest of stone. A knapsack under the head. And a rifle, leaning against the rock.

**Tela:** EntaoAgora entre a prancha 40 (encosta) e a prancha 41 (toca); Mapa2D esquemático do Devil's Den "~40 YARDS" (Selo SCHEMATIC); detalhe do cobertor na vista estéreo de O'Sullivan.

### B09 [7:45] The rifle
Remember the rifle. According to the Library of Congress, it isn't the kind of weapon a sharpshooter would have used. And the same rifle turns up in a number of Gardner's other Gettysburg pictures. It was most likely the photographer's prop, carried from scene to scene. Frassanito also noticed something about the time this all took. A whole series of photographs, roughly an hour of work, spent on a single body. It suggests he may have been one of the last bodies still unburied in that part of the field. Gardner may have felt he was running out of subjects. So he made one. None of this means the soldier wasn't real. He was. He died at Gettysburg, and he was most likely an ordinary infantryman, killed as he climbed that slope. But the man in the most famous photograph of the battle has spent more than a hundred and sixty years being somebody he never was.

**Tela:** detalhe do fuzil na prancha 41; outra foto de Gardner com fuzil semelhante (comparação, sem afirmar além da LoC); DocGrifo `doc_loc_camera` na frase "probably was the photographer's prop".

## Bloco 4: a virada (as legendas)

### B10 [8:45] The words beside the pictures
But the strangest part of this story isn't what Gardner did with his camera. It's what he did with his pen. When the Sketch Book came out after the war, each photograph faced a page of text. For the man by the wall, Gardner wrote this. "The Confederate soldier had built up between two huge rocks, a stone wall, from the crevices of which he had directed his shots." Then he described how the sharpshooter "had laid down upon his blanket to await death." Now turn back one page, to plate forty, the same body on the slope. That page tells a different story. A burial party "found, in a secluded spot, a sharpshooter lying as he fell when struck by the bullet." Lying as he fell. Two plates, one man, two deaths, forty yards apart. And for over a hundred years, almost nobody questioned either one.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_sketchbook_p41` (2 grifos lidos literalmente); DocGrifo `doc_sketchbook_p40` ("a sharpshooter lying as he fell when struck by the bullet"); as duas pranchas lado a lado.

### B11 [9:45] Two armies, one row of dead
And then there are the men from the open field. Remember the shoes. Gardner used one of O'Sullivan's two exposures as plate thirty-six, and he gave it the title that made it famous: "A Harvest of Death." His text says the dead are Confederates. "The rebels represented in the photograph are without shoes." It says they died attacking "an army of patriots," and that "they paid with life the price of their treason." Now look at plate thirty-seven, which Gardner titled "Field where General Reynolds Fell." It's the other exposure, the one from the opposite side. And the text for this one says: "The dead shown in the photograph were our own men." Our own men. Calm, peaceful, looking as if they might rise and keep fighting. When Frassanito matched the bodies, it was the same row of dead. The same hands, the same feet, the same rumpled coat in the grass. And on that coat, a diamond-shaped badge, the kind worn only by the Union Third Corps. The men Gardner sold as traitors were Union soldiers.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_sketchbook_p36` ("The rebels represented in the photograph are without shoes."; "they paid with life the price of their treason"); `foto_colheita_1863` em KenBurns largo; DocGrifo `doc_sketchbook_p37` ("The dead shown in the photograph were our own men."); EntaoAgora de "vista A × vista B" com números de corpos (Selo RECONSTRUCTION, só setas e números sobre as duas fotos reais); Número "3RD CORPS".

### B12 [11:00] Why lie?
Why would he do it? Gardner was a Union man, selling to a Northern audience in the middle of a war. A row of dead Confederates read one way. A row of fallen Union boys read another. And Gardner's title for the second plate was wrong too. General John Reynolds was killed on the first day, in a different part of the battlefield altogether. The Library of Congress, looking back at the Sketch Book, says it plainly: Gardner sometimes rearranged the elements in his photographs or departed from the facts in his writing. The book came out in the years right after the war, when families across the country were still searching for sons who had never come home. Gardner gave them stories. They just weren't always true.

## Bloco 5: o que ainda está lá

### B13 [11:50] November 19th
There's one more line in the sharpshooter's caption, and it's the boldest of all. Remember November 19th. Gardner wrote that on that day he went back to the Devil's Den. "The musket, rusted by many storms, still leaned against the rock, and the skeleton of the soldier lay undisturbed within the mouldering uniform." Four months, and nobody had found him. It's a haunting image. It's also almost certainly invented. By November, burial parties had swept the field, and relic hunters had carried off anything that could be carried. A rifle left in plain sight would never have survived them. But Gardner was right about one thing. He really was at Gettysburg on November 19th. So was a crowd of more than ten thousand people. They had come to dedicate a new cemetery for the Union dead, and the President of the United States had come to say a few words.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_sketchbook_p41` (grifo "The musket, rusted by many storms, still leaned against the rock, and the skeleton of the soldier lay undisturbed within the mouldering uniform"); Número "NOV 19, 1863"; vista do portão do cemitério (1863); Número "10,000+".

### B14 [12:50] Find the President
Eleven days earlier, on November 8th, Abraham Lincoln had sat for a portrait in Gardner's own studio in Washington. This one. Now here is a photograph taken on the morning of November 19th, by a camera from Mathew Brady's studio. It shows the crowd around the speakers' platform, around noon, about three hours before Lincoln spoke. Try to find him. For eighty-nine years, nobody did. The glass plate sat with thousands of other Brady negatives in government hands. Then, in 1952, the chief of the still photo section at the National Archives, a woman named Josephine Cobb, decided to look closer. She found the President's bodyguard, Ward Hill Lamon. She worked out where the crowd was looking. And when she enlarged that corner of the plate, there he was. Right here, in the middle of the crowd. The National Archives calls it the first, and possibly the only, photograph of Lincoln at Gettysburg.

**Tela:** retrato de Lincoln de 08/11/1863 (Gardner); `foto_multidao_lincoln_1863` inteira, lenta; Número "89 YEARS"; foto de Josephine Cobb com uma chapa de Brady (NARA, 1949); zoom final no círculo de Lincoln (crop NARA). Ficha só na data e lugar.

### B15 [13:55] Two minutes
The main speaker that day talked for two hours. Then Lincoln stood up and spoke for about two minutes. Ten sentences. Many people at the back of the crowd didn't hear a word. In one of those sentences, he made a prediction. "The world will little note, nor long remember what we say here, but it can never forget what they did here." He was wrong about the first half. The speech became one of the most famous ever given in English. But he was right about the second half, and that's where Gardner's photographs come back in. Because what they did there is all the camera could ever show. Not the charge up the slope, but the young man who didn't make it to the top. Not the speeches, but the fields that made them necessary.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_discurso_bliss` (grifo literal da frase); páginas das cópias Nicolay/Hay (1863); cartela "~2 MIN · 10 SENTENCES"; plataforma de 1863 (detalhe da chapa de Brady).

### B16 [14:55] What's still out there
So what's still out there today? The dead Gardner photographed are no longer in those fields. Most of the Union dead were moved to the new national cemetery, the one Lincoln dedicated. More than thirty-five hundred are buried there, and nine hundred seventy-nine of them are marked only by a number. The Confederate dead stayed in their field graves for years. Between 1871 and 1873, most of them were dug up and carried south, the majority to Hollywood Cemetery in Richmond, Virginia. And not all of the dead were found. In 1996, erosion near a railroad embankment on the battlefield exposed human bones. Scientists from the Smithsonian confirmed they came from the battle. The man was never identified. In 1997 he was buried in the national cemetery, under a stone that reads simply, "Unknown Civil War Remains."

**Tela:** cemitério nacional (Tipton, séc. XIX) e hoje (CC0/PD); Número "979 UNKNOWN"; Número "1871–1873"; mapa/foto do corte da ferrovia; DocGrifo `doc_nps_2015` ("Unknown Civil War Remains").

### B17 [15:55] The wall is still there
And the sharpshooter's den? You can still stand in it. The two boulders are there. The low stone wall is still there, between them. Park Service photographers have shot it from the same angle as the 1863 plate, and the rocks line up almost perfectly. Visitors climb in and crouch where the young soldier lay. Most of them have no idea he was carried there. As for who he was, nobody knows. A Park Service historian, John Heiser, has argued, from where the fighting took place and from what may be the number 2 on his knapsack, that he was probably a Georgian, from one of the brigades that attacked through the Den. Probably. After a hundred and sixty years, he has no name. He has only the role a photographer gave him.
