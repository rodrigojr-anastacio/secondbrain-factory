## Bloco 1: hook

### B01 [0:00] The staged goodbye
Watch the officer sitting on the fallen palm tree. This is Bikini Atoll, in March 1946. He's Commodore Ben Wyatt, the American military governor of the Marshall Islands, and he's asking the people of this island for something. Their home. Now look at the man in the light shirt who stands up to answer. That's Juda, the leader of Bikini, and he's about to say yes. And here's the strange part. This scene isn't real. The meeting did happen, a month earlier, on a Sunday, after church. What you're watching is a re-enactment, staged for the Navy's cameras just before they left. A Defense Department history sums up that morning in one line. "The 167 Bikinians arrived at Rongerik the next day." One hundred and sixty-seven people. Remember that number.

## Bloco 2: o lugar + a pergunta do título

### B02 [0:46] Two strange things
Bikini is a ring of coral islands around a lagoon, more than two thousand five hundred miles west of Honolulu. You probably know its name from a swimsuit. Today, a handful of caretakers live there. There's no regular way in, no hotel, and the last dive shop closed in 2008. And two things out there are very strange. At the bottom of the lagoon lies a fleet of warships that were sunk on purpose. And in the reef, there's a hole more than a mile wide that wasn't there in 1953.

**Tela:** Mapa2D Honolulu → Bikini "2,500+ MI"; satélite atual; ilha Bikini hoje; NPS Saratoga; cratera Bravo (Sentinel-2).

### B03 [1:22] The question
Those 167 people now have about five thousand four hundred descendants. Some live on a small island called Kili, some in the capital, Majuro, and about fourteen hundred in the United States and abroad. Almost none of them live on Bikini. So why? Why can't you visit Bikini Atoll, and why can't its own people go home to stay? It isn't a military base. The last bomb went off there in 1958. It isn't forgotten. It's a UNESCO World Heritage Site. And the United States once declared it safe, in writing. What keeps it empty is a chain of decisions, a bomb far bigger than its makers expected, and a coconut. Along the way, you'll see a battleship that heard the news of Pearl Harbor, a bomb that missed by a third of a mile, and an aircraft carrier that still has its planes inside.

**Tela:** foto/Número "~5,400"; Kili da ISS; Número "1958"; DocGrifo de página (AEC 1968) no "declared it safe"; Nagato; Able; Saratoga afundando. **Pergunta do título dita em ~1:35.**

## Bloco 3: o que aconteceu ali

### B04 [2:24] Crossroads
In 1946, the United States was the only country with the atomic bomb, and its Navy had one urgent question. What does this thing do to a fleet? To find out, the military planned Operation Crossroads, two atomic tests against real warships, anchored in a real lagoon. More than ninety target ships. Forty-two thousand men. A support fleet of more than a hundred and fifty vessels. On the decks, the Navy left pigs, goats, guinea pigs, rats and mice, to see what would happen to living things. They needed a place far from shipping lanes and cities, with a big, sheltered lagoon. They picked Bikini.

**Tela:** Filme oficial (frota, navios na laguna); fotos da frota-alvo; Números "90+ SHIPS", "42,000 MEN"; cabras no convés; Mapa2D do Pacífico.

### B05 [3:07] A Sunday in February
For centuries, Bikini had belonged to the people who lived on it. Germany and then Japan had ruled the Marshall Islands, and in 1944 the Americans took them. On Sunday, February 10th, 1946, Commodore Wyatt landed in a seaplane. On Bikini, people lived on fish, breadfruit, and coconuts. Remember the coconuts. According to Navy records, Wyatt compared the islanders to the Children of Israel, led by God to the Promised Land. American scientists, he said, were trying to learn how to use the bomb "for the good of mankind and to end all world wars." Then he asked: would they be willing to sacrifice their island for the welfare of all men? The answer from Juda is recorded like this: if it would bring "kindness and benefit to all mankind, my people will be pleased to go elsewhere." Wyatt couldn't promise them money. Only the chance to file claims later.

**Tela:** foto da ilha de 1944; moradores (mulheres e palha); filme da reencenação (Juda); Cartela com a citação (texto de registro citado, Selo "QUOTED FROM NAVY RECORDS"); foto de Wyatt filmando.

### B06 [4:04] Rongerik
On March 7th, the Navy loaded the islanders, their canoes and even the timber of their church onto a landing ship, LST 1108. They were taken about a hundred and forty miles east, to Rongerik, an atoll nobody lived on. Starting on February 26th, Navy builders had put up cisterns, water catchments and twenty-six house frames. The U.S. government's own nuclear lab admits it today. The islanders were "persuaded by the U.S. military to relocate," based on the belief that they would be able to return. Rongerik was smaller, poorer, with fewer fish and fewer coconuts. Within a year, a former Secretary of the Interior, Harold Ickes, wrote that the people were "actually and literally starving to death."

**Tela:** fotos do embarque (LCM, LST 1108, canoa); Mapa2D Bikini → Rongerik "140 MI"; casas de Rongerik; filme dos moradores em Rongerik; DocGrifo LLNL "persuaded".

### B07 [4:50] Kwajalein, then Kili
It got worse. People came down with fish poisoning, and in 1947 a fire swept through Rongerik's coconut trees. In early 1948, an anthropologist, Leonard Mason, found the islanders weak and hungry, and they were moved again, to Kwajalein, where they lived in tents on a strip of grass beside the airport. Then, on November 3rd, 1948, they were resettled on Kili, a single island about four hundred miles southeast of Bikini. No lagoon to fish in, and for months of the year, seas too rough to launch a canoe. That's where most of them would stay.

### B08 [4:54] Able
Back on Bikini, the target fleet moved in. American battleships, cruisers, destroyers and submarines, an aircraft carrier, and captured German and Japanese warships. On July 1st, 1946, at nine in the morning, a B-29 called Dave's Dream dropped the first bomb. It exploded five hundred and twenty feet above the fleet, with the force of about twenty-three thousand tons of TNT. And it missed. The official history says it fell between fifteen hundred and two thousand feet off target. Even so, five ships sank. One of the ships that survived was the USS Independence, a light carrier, left burning and twisted. Reporters were taken out to walk its decks. The German cruiser Prinz Eugen survived both tests, only to capsize months later at Kwajalein. Four days later, in Paris, a designer named Louis Réard showed off a tiny two-piece swimsuit. He named it after the atoll everyone was reading about.

**Tela:** frota-alvo aérea; nuvem Able vista do Fall River; DocGrifo `doc_crossroads_errou_alvo.png`; Número "5 SHIPS SUNK"; frota depois do Able; Número "JULY 5, 1946 · PARIS".

### B09 [5:40] Baker
The second test was the one that changed everything. On July 25th, at eight thirty-five in the morning, the bomb went off ninety feet under the water. A column of seawater shot up into the sky, swallowing the landing ship that had carried the bomb. Then a wall of radioactive mist, about a thousand feet high, rolled across the lagoon at forty-five miles an hour. It bathed almost the entire fleet. That afternoon, at eight minutes past four, the aircraft carrier USS Saratoga, a veteran of the whole Pacific war, slid under. "Radiological conditions prevented any attempt to save the ship." Remember that ship. In the early hours of July 30th, the Japanese battleship Nagato rolled over and sank too. It had been Admiral Yamamoto's flagship. Its radio room was where Japan's admirals first heard that Pearl Harbor had worked.

**Tela:** Filme oficial do Baker (coluna, base surge); fotos Baker (McKinley, aérea); Número "90 FT"; fotos do Saratoga afundando; DocGrifo `doc_crossroads_saratoga.png`; Nagato 1946.

### B10 [6:43] Measured in minutes
Now the Navy had to clean up its ships. Sailors scrubbed the decks with brooms, soap and water, and men with Geiger counters followed behind them. It didn't work. The Navy's report says the safe time aboard some target ships "was measured only in minutes." The ships had become, in the words of one board, radioactive stoves. On August 10th, they stopped working at Bikini and towed the surviving fleet away. A day later, the man in charge of the operation, Vice Admiral William Blandy, admitted the ships could not be made "absolutely safe to board in the near future." A third test was called off. The verdict of the Joint Chiefs' evaluation board was stark. With weapons like these, it is quite possible to depopulate vast areas of the earth's surface, leaving only vestigial remnants of man's material works.

**Tela:** fotos de descontaminação (vassouras, New York, Salt Lake City); Geiger; DocGrifo `doc_crossroads_minutos.png`; Número "AUG 10, 1946"; filme oficial (navios); Cartela citação JCS.

## Bloco 4: por que fechou

### B11 [7:34] Twenty-three
But the strangest part of this story was just getting started. Crossroads was supposed to be a single summer. Bikini stayed a test site for twelve years. The Department of Energy's official list counts them in one line. "Bikini Atoll, twenty-three." Twenty-three nuclear tests, from 1946 to 1958, in four series called Crossroads, Castle, Redwing and Hardtack. Together, they released about seventy-eight megatons. The people of Bikini waited on another island. They waited a long time.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_nv209_23testes.png`; Número "23 TESTS · 1946–1958"; filme/foto Bravo.

### B12 [8:05] Bravo
On March 1st, 1954, the Americans tested a hydrogen bomb on Bikini's reef. They called it Bravo. The report reads: "BRAVO, had a yield of 15 MT," fifteen megatons, "significantly exceeding its expected yield." About two and a half times what its designers expected, and about a thousand times Hiroshima. It left a crater more than a mile wide and as deep as a twenty-story building. Then the wind carried the fallout east. It fell on Rongelap, where sixty-four people lived, on Utirik, on American weathermen on Rongerik, the island where the Bikinians had first been sent, and on a Japanese fishing boat with twenty-three men aboard. Both islands had to be evacuated, a hundred and fifty-seven people from Utirik alone. One of the Japanese fishermen, the boat's radio operator, died that September. Half a century later, in 2005, the State Department told Congress that the United States "still deeply regrets" the Bravo accident.

**Tela:** foto/filme Bravo; DocGrifo `doc_castle_bravo_15mt.png`; Número "15 MEGATONS"; Número "×1,000 HIROSHIMA"; foto aérea Bravo + par cratera; Mapa2D fallout Bikini → Rongelap → Rongerik → Utirik.

### B13 [9:12] Safe
Then, in 1968, the good news. On August 12th, President Lyndon Johnson announced that Bikini could be given back. That same day, the Atomic Energy Commission put it on paper. Its report "declares the atoll once again safe for human habitation." Johnson's goal was to build on those desolate islands "a new and model community." In 1969, a joint task force cleared the debris and the scrub, and replanted coconut trees. New concrete houses went up. By the 1970s, families started coming home. By 1978, about a hundred and forty people were living on Bikini again.

**Tela:** DocGrifo `doc_interior1968_anuncio.png`; DocGrifo `doc_aec1968_seguro.png`; DocGrifo `doc_interior1968_johnson.png`; filme da limpeza de 1969; fotos do retorno (casa, moradores).

### B14 [10:12] The coconut
Now, the coconuts. On Bikini, people were eating what they had always eaten: coconut, pandanus, breadfruit, coconut crab. And all of it was growing in soil that still held cesium-137 from the tests. Government scientists measured the islanders' bodies. Between April 1977 and April 1978, they found "dramatic increases" in cesium. The average was two point three microcuries. The maximum permissible level was three. Several people were over it, and some were close to six. So in August 1978, the government decided to move "the 140 Bikinians in residence at that time" off the atoll. For the second time in thirty-two years, the people of Bikini left their island. This time, the danger was in their food.

**Tela:** fotos do levantamento de 1978 (helicóptero, amostras de peixe, tarrafa); DocGrifo `doc_bnl1979_reevacuacao.png` (dramatic increases / the 140 Bikinians); Número "140"; foto da casa do retorno.

### B15 [11:06] The bill
Then came the money. In 1986, a treaty with the newly self-governing Marshall Islands set aside a hundred and fifty million dollars as a full settlement of all nuclear claims, and created a tribunal to hear them. In 2001, that tribunal ruled on Bikini. It awarded the people "$563,315,500 in compensation." Two hundred and seventy-eight million of that was for the loss of their land, past and future. Another two hundred and fifty million was to clean it up. The fund paid about two point three million. That's about zero point four percent. The Marshall Islands asked Congress for more. In 2005, the State Department answered that there was "no legal basis under the Settlement Agreement for considering additional payments." The Bikinians sued, and lost. In 2009, an appeals court said the 1986 treaty had closed that door: the two countries had agreed to extinguish any judicial jurisdiction over these claims. In 2010, the Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

## Bloco 5: o que ainda está lá

### B16 [12:04] The ground
There's one more reason the island stays empty. It's in the ground. In 2019, a team from Columbia University measured background radiation on Bikini Island at about a hundred and ninety millirem a year. The limit agreed between the two governments is a hundred. In some soil samples, they found americium, a radioactive element left by the bombs, at up to three times the level in soil from the exclusion zone around Chernobyl. Another study found fruit from the island that exceeded even the highest safety limits. The fish in the lagoon are fine. The danger is the soil, and what grows in it. Coconut trees pull the cesium up into the fruit. About ninety percent of the dose there comes from cesium moving from the soil into plants. Government scientists found a fix: spread potassium on the ground, and the trees take up far less cesium. The Department of Energy also advises people there to limit how many coconut crabs they eat. But a fix on paper isn't a town. No community has moved back.

**Tela:** foto ilha Bikini hoje (Sentinel/ISS); Número "190 vs 100 MREM/YR"; foto peixe 1978; DocGrifo LLNL (página); foto retorno casa.

### B17 [12:52] From space
Then there's the crater. This is Bravo, seen from the air in 1954. And this is the same spot from space, in 2025. The hole is still there. A dark blue circle, more than a mile across, in a ring of pale coral. Seventy years later, it's still the easiest way to find Bikini on a satellite map. Astronauts on the International Space Station have photographed it from two hundred and fifty miles up.

**Tela:** EntaoAgora `par_bravo_1954.jpg` × `par_bravo_hoje.jpg`; Sentinel-2 da cratera; Mapa2D com a cratera.

### B18 [13:27] Still on board
And now, the strangest thing on Bikini. Remember the Saratoga. When it sank, it was the oldest American aircraft carrier still afloat. It had fought from Guadalcanal to Iwo Jima. It's still down there, sitting upright on the bottom of the lagoon, in about a hundred and eighty feet of water. Its flight deck is about a hundred feet down. When National Park Service divers surveyed it around 1990, they swam into the hangar deck. They found the planes the Navy had parked there for the test. Three Helldiver dive bombers and an Avenger torpedo bomber, still inside. And the bombs never left either. The divers logged torpedoes, rockets and homing torpedoes. UNESCO lists "stocks of bombs and fuel" in the lagoon as a threat to the site today. Not far away lies Yamamoto's Nagato, upside down. The American battleship Arkansas is upside down too. For twelve years, divers paid to see all this. Then, in 2008, the dive operation shut down.

**Tela:** EntaoAgora `par_saratoga_1946.jpg` × `par_saratoga_hoje.jpg`; desenho NPS do Saratoga (Selo "SURVEY DRAWING"); NPS convés/chaminé/canhão; Número "180 FT"; NPS Nagato canhão; NPS Arkansas.

### B19 [14:32] Kili today
And Kili, the island where Juda's people were sent in 1948? The ocean is coming over it. In 2016, king tides pushed hundreds of people out of their homes there. Hundreds of Bikinians now live on Ejit, a small island in Majuro, and many others have moved to Hawaii and the U.S. mainland. In 2023, the local government's payments stopped, and in January 2024, Kili declared a state of emergency. A lawsuit says the resettlement trust fund, once about sixty million dollars, fell to eighty-nine thousand. The case is still in court.

**Tela:** Kili da ISS; Mapa2D Bikini → Kili "480 MI"; Número "$60 MILLION → $89,002"; filme Rongerik (moradores, sem mortos).

## Bloco 6: fecho

### B20 [15:04] So why?
So why can't you visit Bikini Atoll? Because a Navy wanted to know what an atomic bomb would do to a fleet, and Bikini had the perfect lagoon. Because the tests didn't stop at two, and one bomb turned out two and a half times bigger than planned. Because the island was declared safe, and it wasn't. And because the ground still feeds cesium into the coconuts, and the money meant to fix it ran out.

**Tela:** recap visual: Baker; Bravo; doc AEC 1968; coqueiros/retorno; doc 563 mi.
