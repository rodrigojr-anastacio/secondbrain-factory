Sixty feet under the water off Omaha Beach, there's an American tank. Its gun is still pointing at the beach. On the morning of D-Day, the Army drove it off a boat into the open sea on purpose, because it was built to swim. Twenty-nine of these swimming tanks went in together. Twenty-seven of them never reached the sand, and right behind them came the first wave, with a 19-year-old medic named Charles Shay.

In this video, we follow the call two captains made over a radio, three miles out, and what it left on the bottom of the Channel: the swimming tanks, the warning nobody heeded, a troopship that 2,689 people got off alive, a whole harbor the sea took back in three days, a World War One battleship sunk on purpose, the one swimming tank the U.S. government sold for $50, and the sonar scan that found one of them still aiming at the beach. And what happened to the men who made that call, and to the medic who went back into the surf for the wounded.

How we made this video: we wrote it from the U.S. Army's own histories of D-Day and the 2002 sonar survey of the sunken tanks. The 3D reconstructions of the swimming tanks, the seabed maps (drawn from real elevation and sea-depth data) and the sonar scan reconstruction were all built for this video. The archive film and photos are credited below.
