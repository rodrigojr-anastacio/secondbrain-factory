Right now, under the rice fields of Laos, there are millions of American bombs that never went off. Some are the size of a tennis ball. Some are bigger than a man. They've been there since the 1960s, and they still kill people every year. From 1964 to 1973, America dropped more than two million tons of bombs on Laos, a planeload every eight minutes, on a country it wasn't officially fighting.

In this video: the Navy pilot who was shot down over a country America wasn't at war with, the road the whole Vietnam War ran on, the promise fourteen countries signed in Geneva, the pilots who flew in civilian clothes, the secret radar on top of Phou Pha Thi and the night it fell, the son who climbed a thousand steps to talk to his missing father, the cluster bombs, the woman who told Congress, and why the bombs are still there.

How we made this video: we wrote it from the U.S. Air Force's own records and films, the House hearing of April 22, 2010, the Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency's films from Laos, the Air Force's film about Lima Site 85, and President Obama's speech in Vientiane. The real voices in it are their own: Col. Joe Panza, Rick Holland on the summit of Phou Pha Thi, a DPAA scientist on the mountain, Channapha Khamvongsa and Dr. Robert Keeley before Congress. The strike map shows every American bombing mission recorded over Laos in the Air Force's THOR database. The 3D mountain and the cluster bomb were made for this video from real data. Credits below.
